# Why learn to program?

Today, AI tools write code in seconds. So why spend a day learning to program?

Because writing code is only part of the job. AI is a very fast assistant, but someone needs to know **what to ask for**, **check what came back** and **decide what to do** when something doesn't turn out as expected. That someone is you.

## What is still the programmer's job

![A woman at her laptop, with a checklist next to her and a coffee]({{ '/assets/images/checklist-no-notebook.svg' | relative_url }})
{: .ilustracao .ilustracao-secao }

Illustration: [unDraw](https://undraw.co/)
{: .fs-2 .text-center }

In any project, some parts of the job stay with the programmer. An AI can help with all of them: suggest ideas, remember cases you forgot, explain an error. But you are the one who decides and checks, because everything depends on what **you** want to build and on who is going to use it:

- **Understanding the problem before the solution.** Who will use the app? What does that person want to do? Every project in the guide starts like this, with a plan on paper.
- **Deciding what information to store.** In the Message Board, a message has who wrote it and the text. Does it need anything else, like the date? The choice is yours.
- **Predicting what could go wrong.** What if someone sends an empty message? You decide what counts as an error, and what to do when it happens.
- **Reading and checking the code.** Tools and AIs sometimes create things you didn't ask for. Checking and removing what isn't needed keeps the code simpler to understand.
- **Understanding errors.** When something breaks, the error message says what's missing. Knowing how to read that message is what lets you solve it on your own.

You'll practice each of these parts in the [Message Board]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link projects/message-board/index.md %}) project.

## If you understand it, you can ask clearly

![A woman organizing a list of three items, next to circles that represent ideas]({{ '/assets/images/organizando-um-pedido.svg' | relative_url }})
{: .ilustracao .ilustracao-secao }

Illustration: [unDraw](https://undraw.co/)
{: .fs-2 .text-center }

Knowing how to program also changes the way you ask for help, from an AI or from a person:

- **Asking in small stages.** Instead of asking for the whole app at once, you ask for one part, check it and only then move on. It's the skateboard-to-car idea, in [How projects grow]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link projects/index.md %}#how-projects-grow).
- **Knowing the name of each piece.** A Rails app has pieces with their own names, like router, controller and model. With those names, the request is more precise, and you understand the answer better.

After the workshop, see examples of requests, from the vaguest to the most precise, in [Asking an AI for code]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link extras/asking-ai-for-code.md %}).

## AI as an ally

![A person talking to an AI on the computer]({{ '/assets/images/conversa-com-ia.svg' | relative_url }})
{: .ilustracao .ilustracao-secao }

Illustration: [unDraw](https://undraw.co/)

AI can be a great tutor: it explains an error message, answers a question at any time, shows another way of doing something. See how in [Using AI as a tutor]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link getting-started/ai-as-a-tutor.md %}).

What the guide stands for is that you **understand** what you're building. If you understand it, you can use AI to go faster, without losing control of your own project. In several chapters, the section **Do I need AI for this chapter?** shows what it would be like to ask an AI for that stage and what to check in the result.
